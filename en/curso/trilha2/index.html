🐡 How Fugu works

It isn’t smarter — it’s a manager. Under the hood: a small conductor, a secret pool, decompose→delegate→fuse, the cost, and what the team never saw.

What it is:

Fugu isn’t a new LLM with a better brain. It’s a orchestrator packaged as a model: its “intelligence” lies in coordinating others, not in knowing more.

Why learn:

Clears up the central confusion. Expecting “more IQ” from Fugu leads to frustration; expecting “better management” leads to the right questions—how much does the team cost, and when is it worth it?

Key concepts:

Orchestrator as a model · manager vs. genius · coordination ≠ knowledge.

What it is:

A small “manager” model (the conductor) reads the request and decides: do I solve it myself or assemble a team? It isn’t the specialist—it’s the one who delegates.

Why learn:

It’s the first of the two questions (who handles each part) turned into a model weight. Understanding the conductor explains why Fugu is sometimes fast and sometimes takes minutes.

Key concepts:

Conductor/router · alone vs. team · task-based decision.

What it is:

Behind the endpoint is a pool of frontier models (Opus 4.8, GPT-5.5, Gemini and possibly others). Sakana maintains this proprietary roster; you don’t choose or see who was included.

Why learn:

It’s where the cost and opacity come from. "Opus is in the pool" is a reasonable inference, not a published fact — knowing the difference protects you from marketing.

Key concepts:

Proprietary pool · closed roster · cross-provider · hidden team.

What it is:

Fugu’s cycle: decomposes the task into parts, delegates each one to a specialist in the pool, and merges the responses — all hidden behind a single API call.

Why learn:

It’s the conductor–specialists–fusion operator anatomy from Track 1, now made concrete. Watching the cycle explains why your "1 call" turns into many paid internal calls.

Key concepts:

Decomposition · delegation · merging · 1 endpoint, N internal calls.

What it is:

There’s Fugu and Fugu Ultra (the one tested). Price: US$ 5 in / US$ 30 out per 1M tokens — and orchestration tokens are billed. Launched in Jun 22, 2026.

Why learn:

The output price (US$ 30) is already a warning: the team costs money. Compared with Opus 4.8 (US$ 5 in / US$ 25 out), the output is more expensive — and that cost is multiplied across the whole team.

Key concepts:

Fugu × Fugu Ultra · US$ 5 in / US$ 30 out · orchestration tokens billed.

What it is:

A spoiler on the verdict: across 38 tests, the hidden team never found an answer that a strong model couldn't find on its own. Not once. You paid for the team to get answers the solo model would already have given you.

Why learn:

Close the module with the right tension: the mechanics are elegant, but the question "did it deliver anything extra?" needs proof. Track 2.2 provides the proof.

Key concepts:

Zero marginal gain · pay for orchestration · promise × proof.

📊 The verdict: 38 tests, and when it's worth it

Code-graded field test: Fugu Ultra vs Opus 4.8. 36/38 tie, 4.5× slower, 5× more expensive — and the ladder for deciding when the orchestrator earns its place.

What it is:

38 tasks in 4 waves of increasing difficulty, 334 code-graded calls (hidden tests), identical prompts on both sides, 3–7 runs each, partial credit for multi-rule tasks.

Why learn:

The methodology is what gives the result weight. “Graded by code” removes guesswork: either it passed the test or it didn’t.

Key concepts:

4 waves · code-graded · hidden tests · partial credit · 3–7 runs.

What it is:

Score: 36 ties · 2 Opus wins (soloist) · 0 Fugu wins (team). The hidden team never found an answer the soloist didn’t.

Why learn:

It's the result that punctures the hype: more models didn't mean more correct answers. Orchestration didn't exceed the solo ceiling here.

Key concepts:

36/38 tie · 2 for the soloist · 0 for the team · zero marginal gain.

What it is:

Total: 357 min (Fugu) vs 80 min (Opus) — 4.5× slower. For simple code: Opus 3–6s, Fugu 2–7min for the same answer.

Why learn:

Latency is the invisible cost of fan-out: you wait for the whole team. For interactive work, extra minutes per task add up.

Key concepts:

357 vs 80 min · 4.5× · summed latency · team wait time.

What it is:

Total: US$ 53,60 (Fugu) vs US$ 10,66 (Opus) — 5× more expensive. Most of Fugu's cost comes from the tokens the private team reads and writes (orchestration tokens).

Why learn:

Shows where the money goes: you pay for the whole team, not just the final answer. It’s the hidden cost of Path 1, now with an itemized bill.

Key concepts:

US$ 53,60 vs US$ 10,66 · 5× · orchestration tokens billed.

What it is:

In the 2 tasks that weren’t ties, solo won: a stats module with 6 bugs (Fugu 80% / Opus 100%) and strict Roman numeral validation (Fugu 4/5 / Opus 5/5, accepting "IIII" once).

Why learn:

The team not only failed to win — where there was a difference, it lost. Coordination doesn’t make you immune to slipups; sometimes it adds failure points.

Key concepts:

Stats: 6 bugs, 80% vs. 100% · Roman: 4/5 vs. 5/5 · team failure.

What it is:

A 3-step ladder: (1) Can one strong model already solve it? → use 1 model. (2) No → want 1 endpoint + hedge, and cost/wait time don't matter? → no: 1 model is still the better buy. (3) Yes → the orchestrator earns its place.

Why learn:

It's the practical criterion you take home. In this evaluation, every task was handled by the top of the ladder — but the ladder applies to any future decision, including your Claude Code setup.

Key concepts:

Decision ladder · vendor hedge · 1 endpoint · cost × wait × gain.
