⚖️ Same prompt, N models, one judge

How Fusion works, why it improves results, and what sets it apart from Fugu: parallel ensembling versus breaking a task into pieces.

What it is:

Fusion takes one prompt and sends it, unchanged, to several models at the same time. Each model answers the entire task on its own — nobody receives just one piece.

Why learn:

It’s how Fugu works out of the box. Anyone who confuses "multiple models" with "decomposing the task" understands neither. This is replication, not division.

Key concepts:

Ensemble · fan-out · same prompt · parallel candidates.

What it is:

After N models respond, one model judge reads all the answers and produces the final one — choosing the best, voting, or rewriting a hybrid. It’s the “fusion” in Fusion.

Why learn:

The judge is where the ensemble becomes one answer. Without it, you just have N separate candidates. The quality of the judge is half the quality of Fusion.

Key concepts:

LLM-as-judge · merge · aggregation · pick-best vs. synthesis.

What it is:

Different models make mistakes in different ways. When several answer the same prompt, correct answers tend to coincide while errors spread out—the judge can then favor the consensus.

Why learn:

It’s the theory behind the ensemble: diversity reduces idiosyncratic error. But it only works if the models are truly diverse — identical models make identical mistakes.

Key concepts:

Diversity · uncorrelated errors · consensus · wisdom of crowds.

What it is:

Fugu decomposes: breaks down the task, gives pieces to specialists, and puts them together. Fusion replicates: the same full prompt goes to everyone, and a judge merges competing answers.

Why learn:

These are two opposite answers to the question “who does each part?” Understanding the contrast gives you the vocabulary to classify any orchestrator you come across.

Key concepts:

Decompose vs. replicate · pieces vs. candidates · manager vs. judge.

What it is:

Fusion has a family. Self-consistency: asking the same model several times and voting for the most common answer. Mixture-of-agents: layers of models refining each other's answers.

Why learn:

Recognizing the related approaches shows that “parallel + aggregate” is an old, well-studied pattern — Fusion just packages it across providers behind one API.

Key concepts:

Self-consistency · LLM-as-judge · mixture-of-agents · voting.

What it is:

Running N models means paying for N complete responses — plus the judge call, which reads them all. You spend ≈ N+1 times as much to deliver just one response.

Why learn:

Cost is the ensemble’s Achilles’ heel. Without adding up N+1, "asking everyone" seems free; with the numbers, it’s clear when it’s worth it (and when it isn’t).

Key concepts:

N+1 cost · judge tokens · slowest latency · cost × benefit.

🚀 Fusion in practice and the future of the skill

When the ensemble is worth it, when one strong model is enough, how to build the “judge” pattern yourself, and the mindset for orchestrating with cost awareness.

What it is:

Ensembles shine when errors are costly and there’s no verifiable answer: open-ended decisions, high-stakes writing, and tasks where diverse perspectives catch what a single model would miss.

Why learn:

Defines Fusion’s real niche. Without knowing where it wins, you either apply it to everything (expensive) or nothing (missing the robustness gains).

Key concepts:

Cost of error · no answer key · robustness · high variance.

What it is:

If a top model already solves the task, the ensemble only adds cost and wait time without changing the result — exactly what Fugu’s 38 tests showed (36/38 ties).

Why learn:

It's the honest default. Most tasks "fall to the top of the ladder": 1 strong model ties, faster and cheaper. Orchestrating is the exception, not the rule.

Key concepts:

Ceiling already reached · decision ladder · default = 1 model.

What it is:

You don't need a special API for the Fusion pattern. Ask Claude Code for N answers from different angles and a judge agent to combine them—manual ensemble, full control.

Why learn:

Demystifies Fusion: the pattern is yours, not the product’s. Knowing how to put it together yourself lets you choose when it’s worth running—and see the cost before paying.

Key concepts:

Manual ensemble · judge agent · fan-out + merge · copy-run.

What it is:

The right question isn’t “which model is better?” but “what’s the cost per acceptable result?” Sometimes 3 cheap models + a judge beat 1 expensive model for the same money.

Why learn:

It’s the metric the field test author uses: optimize unit economics, not benchmark vanity. Thinking in $/result changes every orchestration decision.

Key concepts:

Cost per result · cheap×good · quality trade-off · call ROI.

What it is:

Running across multiple providers through one API is insurance: if one model goes offline, gets expensive, or degrades, your application doesn’t stop — it continues with the others.

Why learn:

It's the benefit of Fusion that No depends on extra quality. Even when the ensemble doesn't improve the response, the hedge against lock-in may be worthwhile on its own.

Key concepts:

Vendor lock-in · hedge · resilience · cross-provider.

What it is:

The skill you develop: knowing when orchestrate, which default (decompose vs. ensemble), and which cost — instead of adopting the most automatic product just because it's trendy.

Why learn:

It's the course's destination. The idea is the future; judgment about when to apply it is what separates those who orchestrate well from those who just burn tokens.

Key concepts:

Orchestration judgment · cost awareness · the right pattern · mindset.
